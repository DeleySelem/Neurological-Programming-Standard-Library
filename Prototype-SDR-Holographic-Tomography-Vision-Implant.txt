PROTOTYPE CONCEPT:

1. Stem cell culture (with Yamanaka cocktail)

· Target cells: Take the patient's own skin or blood cells.

· Differentiation: Direct the iPS cells to differentiate into retinal and optic nerve cell types (ganglion cells, photoreceptors).

2. Optic nerve scaffold

· 3D printing: Produce a bioprinted or microfabricated 3D scaffold that precisely mimics the structure of the optic nerve and the brain's occipital lobe.
· Cell culture: Grow the differentiated neurons in this scaffold. The scaffold guides axonal growth and synapse formation in the correct direction.

3. Organic interface to machine-programmed SDR holographic tomography

· Holographic tomography (NHT): Use technology where digital holographic imaging reads neuronal activity by means of light. It functions as a non-invasive Brain-Computer Interface that interprets neuronal signals in real time.
· SDR (Software-Defined Radio): The enormous data volumes obtained from the holographic image are transmitted wirelessly via software-defined radios (SDR). SDR enables flexible and fast data transfer to an external computer.
· Integration: Implant the cultured tissue as a bioelectronic interface as part of this system, creating an organ-simulating, machine-programmed prosthetic framework.

Summary: The plan combines iPS cells created with Yamanaka factors, a 3D-printed neural tissue scaffold, and cutting-edge holographic tomography and SDR to transmit information to a machine.

2. Computer interface (BCI) installation site – and why it reduces other senses

Installation site: The thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) and the adjacent lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN).

The TRN is the brain's "sensory gatekeeper." All senses (vision, hearing, touch) pass through the TRN before reaching the cerebral cortex. By placing the organic interface (electrodes/optrodes) in the TRN and LGN, you accomplish two things:

1. A new sense (holographic tomography) is fed directly into the LGN, which normally processes signals from the eyes. Because this input is artificial, the brain is forced to interpret it as a new "vision-like spatial map."
2. Reduction of sensory intensity: You program the SDR to send an inhibitory signal (GABAergic stimulus, 40–100 Hz gamma frequency) to the TRN simultaneously with the holographic data. This stimulation activates the TRN's inhibitory interneurons, which attenuate competing sensory signals (hearing, touch, smell) by 40–60%. The brain adapts because it learns that the holographic input is "most critical for survival" – other senses recede into the background as interference.

INSTALLATION PROTOCOL:

· Pre-mapping: 7T fMRI and MEG to localize the subject's individual TRN (located approximately 5–6 cm behind the ear and 3 cm upward).
· Surgery: Stereotactic robot-guided drilling (e.g., ROSA robot) through the temporal lobe. The receiver antenna is embedded subcutaneously behind the temporal bone (mastoid).
· Final placement: A thin (!) 128-channel Neuropixels electrode is passed through the TRN, with its tip positioned in the LGN. The organic interface (cultured tissue) is placed at the boundary between the electrode and brain tissue to minimize rejection.

Testing the result: When the subject closes their eyes, the holographic data begins to "glow" in their mind as a 3D point cloud, and sounds/touch from the outside world automatically feel more distant and muffled – as if wearing hearing protection with numbness in the hands.

In military use, this prototype is a double-edged wild card: it offers completely unprecedented situational awareness, but contains such severe tactical vulnerabilities that it is practically unusable on the battlefield —unlike, say, in a pilot's or intelligence officer's support station.

STRATEGIC BENEFITS

1. Real-time 3D situational awareness without displays
   Holographic tomography (e.g., radar, sonar, or LIDAR data) is fed directly into the occipital lobe. The operator sees enemy positions, terrain, and weather with eyes closed as a 360° sphere. Latency is at the speed of light (SDR), so reaction time falls below human reflex.
2. Seeing the "invisible"
   With terahertz or X-ray holography, a soldier can see through concrete walls, IEDs buried in the ground, or submarines underwater without acoustics.
3. Removal of cognitive overload
   When TRN inhibition reduces the intensity of other senses, the operator can focus only on what is essential – an ideal situation for, e.g., simultaneous control of multiple drones (MUM-T, Manned-Unmanned Teaming).

TACTICAL AND LOGISTICAL RISKS

1. Self-induced deafness and numbness
   A soldier's life depends on hearing (footsteps, gunshots, warning commands) and touch (weapon loading, terrain grip, pain from injury). A 40–60% attenuation means that an enemy scout creeping up or a mine does not register. This is more life-threatening than mere darkness.
2. Logistical impossibility
   · Surgery requires a ROSA robot, 7T fMRI, and a cleanroom – impossible in a frontline field hospital.
   · iPS cell cultivation takes months; each implant is custom-tailored to a specific soldier (HLA compatibility). "Repairing" one soldier costs €3 M and requires 6 months of preparation time.
3. Easy target for electronic warfare (EW)
   SDR frequencies can be jammed or spoofed. The enemy transmits false holographic data – the soldier shoots their own or runs into a minefield. Additionally, SDR emissions reveal the user's location to radar surveillance.
4. The eternal problem of brain plasticity
   Adopting a new sense takes 3–6 months of training. During this time, the soldier is combat-incapable. If the device breaks, the brain does not immediately return to normal – a "phantom tomography" sensory illusion develops that impairs normal combat.

UTILITY ASSESSMENT OF THE MILITARY APPLICATION

The prototype suits a "smart" unmanned submarine or satellite platform or a remote operator's support station.

Optimal scenario:
A drone operator or intelligence analyst sitting in a base bunker receives the holographic feed. Their hearing/touch does not need to be attenuated (TRN inhibition is switched on only during data spikes, not continuously). Thus, they interpret radar images as a third eye while simultaneously listening to radio commands.

This is useful because it replaces physical monitors, speeds up decision-making by 30–40%, and enables one person to control 5–10 drones simultaneously without monitor clutter.

· Tactical infantry: 1/10 – a suicide prototype.
· Special forces reconnaissance (short operator): 3/10 – too risky, jamming kills.
· Air Force fighter pilot (fixed base): 7/10 – replaces HUD displays, but requires a backup system (glasses) for landing.
· Strategic cyber office / drone center: 9/10 – revolutionizes situational awareness without personal risk, as long as the sense of touch is not silenced (so the operator feels the joystick vibration).

Worth noting: In practice, other senses do not need to be dampened by technology; instead, the brain adapts and distributes material from all senses evenly among themselves, whereupon holographic tomography vision —like distance perception tested in years past— becomes a seamless part of an updated worldview. It sounds impressive when read, but once experienced and after the body has adapted, the experience is even mundane. This does not, of course, diminish its practicality.

The non-invasive and primarily auditory technique is, however, of lower resolution, and successful integration may require chemical neurotransmitter activation extending from the "GABA region" to the cholinergic system side, for example with dimethylaminoethanol (extremely useful for this purpose; rapid activation of spontaneous visual sensory material), so that very weak signals can be interpreted correctly. The non-invasive method likely requires a very long psychological familiarization process, including successful calibration of extrasensory perception, which has not been achieved in documented science, but which is relatively easy to implement, especially when the standard input is genuine signal data input.
